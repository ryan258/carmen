## A short, repeatable learning session

1. Choose one case from the table. Read its three evidence notes and question. Work without a timer; pause whenever useful. A partner can read aloud.
2. Say, point to, or write the answer. Name two pieces of evidence and explain why one tempting alternative fails. No typing is required.
3. Open one hint at a time if needed, then open the worked answer. Identify the mistake or inference, not just the answer label.
4. Try the transfer question before opening its answer. On a later session, try it again without the original case.
5. Keep an optional local note: case ID, evidence used, hint level, transfer explanation, and next question. No account, score export or new tracking feature is implied.

An educator can assign a case ID and this guide’s date/content version. The complete prompt is here; students do not need to inspect JSON or visit external sources to solve it. Open source links for a separate source-evaluation activity. The collapsed answer sections keep explanations out of the initial reading; expand them when preparing an answer copy. ## 1. The Producer Rule

**Downtown Square · `classic-downtown-square` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Select the one record satisfying two conditions and explain why matching only one is insufficient.

In this fictional training case, a training market bell vanished from ACME's imagined outdoor market exercise on the Square. Carmen is already gone, but The Square Dealer is still in Bentonville, leaving the evidence below for ACME to examine.

Apply both the receipt and the producer rule to the fictional register. All information needed is in the clue sheets.

1. Real-world context: the outdoor Bentonville Farmers Market uses the Square. Its operator describes a producer-only market: sellers grow, raise, or make their goods. Fictional receipt: cut flowers, bought directly from the grower at a market stall.
2. Fictional stall register: Fern grows cut flowers at stall 12; Basil grows herbs at stall 18; Reed resells cut flowers from another grower at stall 24; Iris makes pressed-flower cards at stall 30. The bell was left beside the stall matching the receipt.
3. A fictional witness saw the courier remain among the market stalls wearing the recorded disguise: Farmers market vendor. The names and stall numbers are invented, not real vendor listings.

**Which stall matches both what was bought and who produced it?**

- **A.** Iris, stall 30
- **B.** Fern, stall 12
- **C.** Basil, stall 18
- **D.** Reed, stall 24

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Check two conditions: the item must be cut flowers, and the seller must have grown them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Reed has cut flowers but did not grow them. Basil and Iris produce different goods.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: Fern, stall 12. Fern grows the cut flowers that the receipt describes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: Fern, stall 12.**

Fern meets both conditions: cut flowers and a seller who grew them. Basil meets the producer rule but sells herbs. Reed sells the right item but fails the direct-from-grower condition. Iris makes her goods, but a pressed-flower card is not the cut flowers on the receipt. These are invented records; they do not accuse any real vendor of breaking a rule. The receipt and witness place the hideout at Farmers Market stalls; the witness identifies the Farmers market vendor disguise.

Warrant: **Farmers Market stalls**; disguise: **Farmers market vendor**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: Downtown Bentonville Inc. describes its Farmers Market as a place to buy goods from the people who grow, raise, or make them. Offerings vary by season.

</details>

**Transfer question:** A different fictional receipt says “pressed-flower card, bought from its maker.” Which stall now fits? Explain why the former answer no longer does.

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

Iris, stall 30. The required product changed; Iris makes those cards, while Fern grows cut flowers. Both product and producer conditions still matter.

</details>

**Source and claim ledger:** [Bentonville Farmers Market — location and producer policy](https://downtownbentonville.org/events/farmers-market) — Downtown Bentonville Inc.

FAQ: “Where is the market located?”, “What can I buy at the market?” and “Are the vendors local?” These passages establish the Square setting, flowers among possible goods and the producer rule. The page separately lists indoor and Wednesday venues, so the case explicitly uses an imagined outdoor Square market, without dates or promised inventory.

**Review limits:** No observation of real vendors or on-site map verification. This is a two-condition reading problem, not evidence of vendor compliance.

**Fiction boundary:** All suspects, accusations, witnesses, costumes, stolen items, props, records and proposed plans are invented. Source notes establish the real setting or factual account; they do not document these events. The warrant follows the fictional records.

## 2. Two Stores, Two Dates

**Walmart Museum · `classic-walmart-museum` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Calculate a difference between historical years while keeping each date attached to the correct place and event.

In this fictional training case, a replica timeline card vanished from ACME's fictional history display at Walton's 5&10. Carmen is already gone, but The Price Roller is still in Bentonville, leaving the evidence below for ACME to examine.

Keep the two store identities separate. Calculate the difference between opening years, not an exact anniversary interval.

1. Historical fact card, based on Walmart's account: Sam Walton opened Walton's 5&10 in Bentonville in 1950. The first Walmart opened in Rogers in 1962.
2. Fictional filing rule: subtract the earlier opening year from the later one, then name the city of the later opening. A delivery slip says the accomplice stayed at the earlier store, not the later store.
3. A fictional witness followed the accomplice to Walton's 5&10. Costume inventory identifies the disguise as Dime-store clerk. The filing rule and replica card are ACME inventions.

**Which entry gives the year difference and the city of the later opening?**

- **A.** 12 years; Bentonville
- **B.** 13 years; Rogers
- **C.** 10 years; Rogers
- **D.** 12 years; Rogers

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

First identify which event came later; then subtract the two years.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

1962 is later than 1950. The later store was in Rogers; count elapsed years, not both endpoints.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: 12 years; Rogers. 1962 minus 1950 equals 12.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: 12 years; Rogers.**

1962 − 1950 = 12, and Rogers is the city of the later opening. '12 years; Bentonville' gets the subtraction right but assigns the earlier store's city to the later event. '13 years; Rogers' counts both labeled years instead of their difference. '10 years; Rogers' rounds to a decade and loses two years. This compares opening years, not exact opening dates. For the warrant, the delivery slip points back to the earlier store, Walton's 5&10; the witness confirms that place and the Dime-store clerk disguise.

Warrant: **Walton's 5&10**; disguise: **Dime-store clerk**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: Walton's 5&10 in Bentonville and the first Walmart in Rogers are distinct stores in Walmart's historical account.

</details>

**Transfer question:** A fictional library opened in Eastbank in 1984, and its branch opened in Westbank in 1991. Give the difference between the opening years and the later branch’s town.

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

7 years; Westbank. 1991 minus 1984 is 7. Counting both endpoint labels would give 8; naming Eastbank would mix up the events. No exact anniversary is established.

</details>

**Source and claim ledger:** [Sam Walton — The Road to Walmart](https://corporate.walmart.com/about/sam-walton) — Walmart.

“The Road to Walmart”, paragraph beginning with the 1950 move to Bentonville. It distinguishes Walton’s 5&10, Bentonville, 1950 from the first Walmart, Rogers, 1962. The arithmetic is our derivation from those years. The page is the company’s own account; its promotional judgments are not adopted.

**Review limits:** No claim about present exhibit layout, exact opening-day intervals, first-ever entrepreneurial activity, or the social impact of the business.

## 3. A Route Through the Evidence

**Crystal Bridges · `classic-crystal-bridges` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Check a route one connection at a time, including a closed-link constraint and a required destination.

In this fictional training case, a replica transfer card vanished from ACME's imaginary museum-route exercise. Carmen is already gone, but The Varnish is still in Bentonville, leaving the evidence below for ACME to examine.

Use only the open links on the fictional route sheet. Each arrow must follow one listed connection.

1. Real-world context: Crystal Bridges describes two spring-fed ponds crossed by bridge structures, with gallery and studio pavilions around them. In this fictional exercise, a crate was left in the Bridge pavilion, the room crossing water.
2. Fictional route sheet: Entrance connects to Gallery; Gallery connects to Bridge pavilion; Bridge pavilion connects to Studio. These links work both ways. The direct Entrance–Studio link is closed, and there are no other links. A witness saw the courier stop at the water-crossing room.
3. The fictional costume record says Art handler. The route sheet is an invented network for reasoning, not the museum floor plan or an accessibility route.

**Which route starts at Entrance, visits Bridge pavilion, and ends at Studio without using a closed or missing link?**

- **A.** Entrance → Gallery → Bridge pavilion → Studio
- **B.** Entrance → Bridge pavilion → Gallery → Studio
- **C.** Entrance → Studio → Bridge pavilion → Studio
- **D.** Entrance → Gallery → Studio → Bridge pavilion

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Check each consecutive pair of rooms, not just whether all the right room names appear.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The only open first step from Entrance is Gallery. Gallery has no direct link to Studio.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: Entrance → Gallery → Bridge pavilion → Studio. All three steps use open links.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: Entrance → Gallery → Bridge pavilion → Studio.**

Entrance → Gallery → Bridge pavilion → Studio follows three listed open links in order and finishes at Studio. The route starting Entrance → Bridge pavilion invents a link; Gallery → Studio is also missing. The route starting Entrance → Studio uses the closed link. The route ending at Bridge pavilion both invents Gallery → Studio and fails the required destination. The crate record and witness identify Bridge pavilion as the hideout; the costume record gives Art handler. This proves a path only in the fictional network, not a real museum route.

Warrant: **Bridge pavilion**; disguise: **Art handler**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: Architect Moshe Safdie designed Crystal Bridges to relate to its Ozark setting; the museum describes ponds, bridges and pavilions as parts of that design.

</details>

**Transfer question:** In the same fictional network, Gallery–Bridge pavilion now closes and Entrance–Studio opens. Can you still reach Bridge pavilion from Entrance? Give an allowed path.

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

Yes: Entrance → Studio → Bridge pavilion. The original links work both ways, so Studio can connect back to Bridge pavilion. This answer follows the changed network, not a memorized original route.

</details>

**Source and claim ledger:** [Architecture — Moshe Safdie](https://crystalbridges.org/architecture) — Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art.

“Architecture” introduction and “Moshe Safdie” section. The latter identifies the architect, natural ravine, two spring-fed ponds, bridge structures and surrounding pavilions. These are the factual setting; the room labels, connections and closures are invented.

## 4. What Counts as Adaptive Reuse?

**The Momentary · `classic-the-momentary` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Apply a two-part definition to a new proposal and distinguish reuse from continued use, demolition and replication.

In this fictional training case, a replica building-use card vanished from ACME's imaginary design workshop at the Momentary. Carmen is already gone, but The Pop-Up is still in Bentonville, leaving the evidence below for ACME to examine.

Adaptive reuse gives an existing building a different use while retaining substantial parts of it. Apply both parts of that idea.

1. Real-world context: the Momentary reused a former cheese factory for contemporary arts. Its account says the architects kept most of the building, adding elements as needed. Fictional brief: keep the existing structure and give it a new use.
2. The venue describes Fermentation Hall as a black box theater. In the fictional case, a sound-check record places the handoff in that theater; a second note names the hall whose name recalls cheese-making.
3. A fictional witness placed the courier in the hall named for the factory's former work, wearing the recorded disguise: Stage technician. The four project proposals are invented comparisons, not real construction plans.

**Which fictional proposal follows the approach described in the brief?**

- **A.** Demolish the factory and build a new performance venue
- **B.** Build a replica factory elsewhere for performances
- **C.** Keep the factory structure and adapt it for performances
- **D.** Keep the factory structure and continue making cheese

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Look for both an existing structure retained and a different use.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Keeping the building alone is insufficient; a new arts venue alone is also insufficient.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: Keep the factory structure and adapt it for performances. It preserves the building while changing its use.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: Keep the factory structure and adapt it for performances.**

Keeping the factory structure and adapting it for performances meets both conditions. Continuing cheese production keeps the structure but not a new use. Demolition creates a new arts venue but removes the existing building. A replica elsewhere copies an appearance without reusing this structure. The sound-check record and witness point to Fermentation Hall; the witness identifies Stage technician. The case does not claim a measured carbon saving or that all original features were retained.

Warrant: **Fermentation Hall**; disguise: **Stage technician**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: The Momentary names its conversion from a cheese plant to an arts space as an adaptive reuse project by Wheeler Kearns Architects.

</details>

**Transfer question:** A fictional town keeps an old railway depot’s main structure and turns it into a library. Does it fit this definition of adaptive reuse? Name both reasons.

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

Yes: a substantial existing structure is retained, and the use changes from railway depot to library. Repairing it to continue as a depot would satisfy only the retention condition.

</details>

**Source and claim ledger:** [About the Momentary — Our Space and Fermentation Hall](https://themomentary.org/about/) — The Momentary.

“You Belong Here” describes the former cheese factory and contemporary arts use. “Our Space” names Wheeler Kearns Architects and describes substantial retention. “Fermentation Hall” identifies the black box theater and its cheese-factory naming connection.

**Review limits:** No operating-hours, admission, floor-area or quantified environmental claim. This small task teaches building reuse, not the full history of the region or its Indigenous communities.

In this fictional ACME training case, a replica landmark label disappeared in Buenos Aires. Carmen has left the city; Tito Trapaza left two record fragments. Use them to check a catalog entry.

Compare the intersection, year and architect. Use the written facts; no local knowledge or picture recognition is needed.

2. City fact card: Alberto Prebisch designed the Obelisco, erected in 1936. A second fictional fragment says the handoff is at that monument, not simply anywhere along either avenue.
3. The fictional costume inventory identifies Stagehand. The four catalog entries are deliberately mixed-up practice records; incorrect entries are not alternative historical accounts.

**Which proposed entry preserves all three verified details?**

- **A.** Corrientes and 9 de Julio; 1936; architect not recorded
- **B.** Corrientes only, intersection unknown; 1936; Alberto Prebisch
- **C.** Corrientes and 9 de Julio; 1936; Alberto Prebisch
- **D.** Corrientes and 9 de Julio; 1836; Alberto Prebisch

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Check place, date and attribution separately. One correct detail does not validate the whole entry.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

One entry shifts the date by a century; another loses the architect; another loses the intersecting avenue.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: Corrientes and 9 de Julio; 1936; Alberto Prebisch. It preserves every detail on the fact cards.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

The entry with Corrientes and 9 de Julio, 1936 and Alberto Prebisch preserves all three details. The 1836 entry shifts the year by 100. </details>

## 6. Stay Within the Evidence

**Mendoza · `puente-inca` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Choose a conclusion proportionate to a source and identify unsupported exclusivity, name-based inference and false precision.

In this fictional ACME training case, a replica field notebook disappeared from an ACME desk in Mendoza. Vera Vicario left a visitor-text summary and several proposed conclusions. The regional hideout is outside the city stop; the game marker is not a field route.

Choose the conclusion supported by the source note. Do not turn a limited summary into a complete scientific explanation.

1. Source note: the Argentine government visitor text for Uspallata links the formation of Puente del Inca to hot springs and erosion in a mountain climate. This short visitor text does not give a complete geological history.
2. Fictional notebook: the handoff is at the feature described by those two natural processes. A second fictional message names Puente del Inca. Neither record supplies a formation date or evidence of human construction.
3. The fictional costume label says Geology assistant. A place name, a guess about its age, or a single attractive explanation cannot replace evidence.

**Which conclusion can ACME support from the supplied text?**

- **A.** The account links formation to both hot springs and erosion
- **B.** The account proves hot springs were the only process
- **C.** The name proves people built the feature
- **D.** The account establishes an exact formation year

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Separate what the text actually supports from what a reader might assume.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

The source names two processes. It does not establish a builder or an exact formation year.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: The account links formation to both hot springs and erosion. This preserves both factors without claiming the text explains everything.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: The account links formation to both hot springs and erosion.**

The supported conclusion retains both hot springs and erosion and attributes the explanation to the supplied account. Saying hot springs were the only process drops erosion and adds an unsupported exclusion. Inferring human construction from a name is not evidence. An exact formation year cannot be recovered from a text that gives none. This does not settle the full geology or the history of the place name. The two fictional messages identify Puente del Inca as the regional hideout; the costume label gives Geology assistant.

Warrant: **Puente del Inca**; disguise: **Geology assistant**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: The government visitor account describes Puente del Inca using natural processes. Establishing its full formation history would require more detailed geological evidence.

</details>

**Transfer question:** A fictional science note says frost and running water helped shape a rock surface. Someone summarizes it as “water alone did it in exactly ten years.” What two overclaims need correction?

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

“Alone” drops frost and excludes other influences without evidence. “Exactly ten years” adds a duration absent from the note. A defensible summary preserves both named processes and leaves the duration unknown.

</details>

**Source and claim ledger:** [Uspallata, Mendoza — Qué visitar: Puente del Inca](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/node/465223) — Government of Argentina.

Government of Argentina, “Uspallata, Mendoza”, “¿Qué visitar?”, Puente del Inca paragraph. It attributes formation to hot springs and erosion in a mountain climate. The Spanish passage was read and paraphrased in English. This supports a limited account of contributing processes, not a complete mechanism or age.

**Review limits:** The page’s distance in “¿Cómo llegar?” is for Uspallata, not Puente del Inca, and is not reused. Provincial pages and the management-plan PDF could not be consistently fetched, so their search snippets were not used as review evidence. Exact mineral chemistry, age, Indigenous naming history and the map marker remain outside this review.

**Fiction boundary:** All suspects, accusations, witnesses, costumes, stolen items, props, records and proposed plans are invented. Source notes establish the real setting or factual account; they do not document these events. The warrant follows the fictional records.

## 7. Reconstruct the Rail Log

**Salta · `classic-salta` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Reorder records by time and distinguish the city connection from the train segment.

In this fictional ACME training case, a model locomotive disappeared from an ACME display in Salta. Saúl Sandoval mixed three pages of a made-up rail log. The viaduct hideout is regional, not at Salta city's map marker.

Put the fictional entries in chronological order. Read the timestamps, not the order of the clue sheets. These are not booking times or a travel timetable.

1. Fictional entry, 13:00: the train returned to San Antonio de los Cobres without the suspect. The suspect had stayed at La Polvorilla Viaduct. These times are invented for the puzzle.
2. Fictional entry, 09:00: depart San Antonio de los Cobres by train. Separate context: the operator describes a road connection from Salta and a train leg to La Polvorilla; Salta city is not the rail boarding point in that itinerary.
3. Fictional entry, 11:00: the train visited La Polvorilla Viaduct. The witness saw the suspect remain there in a Train conductor disguise. All three entries belong to the same day.

**Which sequence follows the three timestamps?**

- **A.** La Polvorilla → San Antonio de los Cobres → San Antonio de los Cobres
- **B.** San Antonio de los Cobres → San Antonio de los Cobres → La Polvorilla
- **C.** Salta city → La Polvorilla → Salta city
- **D.** San Antonio de los Cobres → La Polvorilla → San Antonio de los Cobres

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Find the earliest of the three times before looking at where that page appears.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

09:00 is departure, 11:00 is the viaduct visit, and 13:00 is return. Departure and return share a station.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 3</summary>

Answer: San Antonio de los Cobres → La Polvorilla → San Antonio de los Cobres. Sort 09:00, 11:00, then 13:00.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer and warrant</summary>

**Answer: San Antonio de los Cobres → La Polvorilla → San Antonio de los Cobres.**

09:00 comes before 11:00, which comes before 13:00, so the train leaves San Antonio de los Cobres, visits La Polvorilla, and returns. Starting with La Polvorilla puts the visit before departure. Putting both station entries first moves the return before the visit. Replacing the station with Salta city confuses the city connection with the rail leg and contradicts the log. The 11:00 witness and 13:00 absence both place the suspect at La Polvorilla Viaduct; the witness supplies Train conductor. The invented times say nothing about real journey duration.

Warrant: **La Polvorilla Viaduct**; disguise: **Train conductor**. The location field is the established game stop; regional hideouts do not become city-center destinations.

Factual context: The operator itinerary separates road and train legs: rail boarding at San Antonio de los Cobres, a visit to La Polvorilla, and a return to that station.

</details>

**Transfer question:** Fictional log, same day: 15:20 arrive at Lake stop; 14:05 depart Depot; 16:10 return to Depot. Put the events in order, then say whether these times tell you a real train schedule.

<details>
<summary>Transfer answer and reasoning</summary>

Depot at 14:05 → Lake stop at 15:20 → Depot at 16:10. They establish order inside the fictional log only, not real service times or journey duration.

## 8. Part and Whole

**Córdoba · `jesuit-block` · content version 4**

Learning objective: Distinguish a component from a larger heritage property and use evidence to select the correct geographic scale.

In this fictional ACME training case, a replica catalog card disappeared from an ACME teaching collection in Córdoba. Clara Cortázar left a heritage summary and a city delivery record. All theft and suspect details are invented.

A heritage property may include several separate places. Keep the city block distinct from the wider listed property.

1. UNESCO fact card: the Jesuit Block in Córdoba city includes a university, church, residence and college. The World Heritage property also includes five estancias, historically farming estates, in the province.
2. Fictional delivery note: the handoff stayed at the city block with the university and college. A second receipt places the suspect at that block, not on a trip to one of the estates.
3. The fictional costume register identifies Professor. The puzzle asks which part of the listed property the record describes, not who deserves sole credit for its history.

**Which interpretation fits both the UNESCO summary and the fictional delivery record?**

- **A.** The handoff was outside the listed property because only the estancias count
- **B.** The handoff was at the urban Jesuit Block, one part of the wider property
- **C.** The handoff was at all five estancias because they are one listed property

**Answer: The handoff was at the urban Jesuit Block, one part of the wider property.**

The two fictional delivery records support Jesuit Block as the hideout; the costume register gives Professor. Heritage status describes a recognized property, not an endorsement of every aspect of its past.
